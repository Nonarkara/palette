      Read the interval

      Sanzo Wada arranged 159 named colours into 348 combinations. The unit is the relationship. Red changes beside blue. Pale yellow changes again when it becomes the smallest part of a quartet.

      This edition makes each relationship large enough to inhabit. Search is a question, chance is a method, and the index is the map back out.

      阅读色彩之间的间距

      和田三造将159种具名色彩编排成348组配色。真正的单位不是单一颜色，而是颜色之间的关系。红色与蓝色并置后会改变；浅黄色在四色组合中占据最小面积时，也会承担新的作用。

      和田三造

      SANZO
      WADA

      1883 — 1967

      He did not leave us a trend. He left a way of seeing relationships.

      Sanzo Wada moved across oil painting, colour research, teaching, kimono, woodblock print, stage, and film. That range matters here. His colour work was not theory sealed inside a book; it belonged to a life spent testing how visual relationships behave on bodies, paper, screens, and in public culture.

      In 1927 he founded the Japan Standard Color Association, now the Japan Color Research Institute. Its research led to Shikimei Sōkan in 1931 and Haishoku Sōkan in 1933–34—the source later republished as A Dictionary of Color Combinations.

      This exhibition is Dr Non’s interpretation of that method. A colour is never asked to perform alone. It gains force through adjacency, proportion, material, and use. We preserve the relationships, credit the record, and keep our interface, role assignments, and digital readings visibly separate from Wada’s own work.

      2. 1927 Colour as institution

        He establishes the Japan Standard Color Association and gives colour research an enduring public structure.

      3. 1933–34 Relationships catalogued

        Haishoku Sōkan publishes combinations rather than isolated swatches: colour made legible through company.

        The Japanese government names him a Person of Cultural Merit.

      CURATORIAL POSITION / Palette is an independent digital exhibition conceived, interpreted, and built by Dr Non Arkaraprasertkul after the colour research of Sanzo Wada. Historical facts remain attributed to their records; all interpretive prose and software classifications remain Dr Non’s.

      Why I keep looking

      Non Arkaraprasertkul is an architect, urban anthropologist, and civic technologist based in Bangkok. His path runs from KMITL and MIT through Oxford and Harvard, ten years of fieldwork in Shanghai, and live municipal systems in Thailand.

      Across buildings, cities, software, and colour, the same question returns: how can a complex system become legible without becoming simplistic? Palette is one answer. It turns a historical catalogue into a room for looking slowly, comparing relationships, and testing taste against evidence.

      The research position

      This exhibition does not argue that taste is universal. It is a visual research instrument: a way to make historic relationships large, searchable, comparable, and open to disagreement. The digital readings describe this software’s method, not Wada’s intention or the exact appearance of printed ink.
